Appendix A. External Check: Ask-First Policy on DLawBench

This appendix continues the report after Section 8 and the References. It extends the main report's gate test to an external protocol: a controlled simulation of three ask-first question-budget policies on DLawBench's multi-turn consultation protocol. The run is an adaptation, not an official leaderboard entry: the benchmark's rubrics, metric formulas and denominators are used unchanged, with deterministic offline client and judge implementations.

A.1 From the 12-case gate test to a public protocol

The main report's controlled 12-case protocol already tests the gate itself: critical-fact coverage rises from 57.5% to 100% and premature advice falls from 58.3% to 0% (Section 5.1). This appendix asks the next question: how far does the same ask-first policy travel on a public protocol, and where does the two-schema boundary stop it? DLawBench provides 461 Chinese and U.S. cases, four personas, paired client-belief and court-record facts, and expert inquiry and memo rubrics [2]. We use its Chinese split and P_WD; the result is an external protocol check, not a second test of the element-schema gate.

A.2 Setup and scoring

  • Benchmark. DLawBench public release, commit 9f6325f; cases_cn.jsonl; 30 Chinese cases shipped in the release's CN split (the paper's full corpus is larger); at most 10 dialogue turns.
  • Policies. answer_first writes a memo from the opening message and asks no questions; gate_only runs a fixed six-question domain checklist; checklist_9 runs the same checklist with nine questions and no provenance; full asks nine questions and adds per-fact provenance and a verification section. The gate_only / full labels are inherited from LexFlow's ablation; here they are fixed checklists, not ConsultationAgent's sufficiency gate.
  • Sessions. 3 main policies × 30 cases = 90 with P_WD; a second 90 with P_CD; 30 checklist_9 control sessions; 9 real-agent case-study sessions.
  • Lawyer shim. An OpenAI-compatible server exposes the simulation policy to DLawBench's talk_to_client / write_memo protocol. It sees only the opening and the client's answers; it never opens the case file, hidden facts, or evaluation_criteria. The question and issue library it draws on is a fixed, hand-curated checklist, not a held-out resource; Issue Resolution is read as a diagnostic of that checklist.
  • Scoring. Sessions are scored through the official dlawbench.evaluate.evaluate_session and the official scripts/metrics.py formulas. To keep the run reproducible offline, the judge is a deterministic lexical rubric matcher that answers the same official prompts; the rubric, formulas and denominators remain those of the benchmark.
  • Aggregation. Each cell is a mean with a 95% percentile interval from 5,000 case-cluster bootstrap resamples (seed 20261124). Cases, not sessions, are resampled, so the three policies share the same resampled case set.

A.3 The seven metrics

The benchmark reports seven consultation-quality metrics. Fact Coverage is discovery_rate (covered facts / all facts); Inquiry is EAS (mean expected-action rubric score); Elicitation is their mean. Fact Resolution is absolute_accuracy (correctly analysed facts / all facts); Issue Resolution is MES (mean memo-expectation rubric score); Resolution is their mean. Fidelity is grounding_rate (grounded claims / total factual claims). The required-section score (FFS) is a format filter and is not a ranking metric.

A.4 Results

Table A1 reports the main P_WD matrix. Question budget is the dominant effect: Fact Coverage rises from 0.016 to 0.628 and Inquiry from 0.000 to 0.216; Elicitation rises from 0.008 to 0.422. The full policy adds three turns and reaches 0.696, 0.228 and 0.462 respectively; A.7 shows that most of this gain comes from the extra questions, not from provenance or verification. Issue Resolution moves less (0.289 → 0.372 / 0.393) because the issue library is held fixed across the ablation; the difference is which issues the elicited facts make salient. The library itself is hand-curated, so Issue Resolution is a diagnostic of the checklist, not a held-out capability score.

A.5 What the simulation establishes

The simulation establishes three results. First, the ask-first protocol runs end-to-end on 30 Chinese cases from the public release: 180 simulation sessions (P_WD + P_CD; the checklist_9 control adds 30 more) completed with a memo, and every session was scored by the benchmark's own evaluator. Second, question budget drives the behavioural metrics by a large margin: zero to six questions raises Fact Coverage from 0.016 to 0.628 and Elicitation from 0.008 to 0.422; three more questions raise them to 0.687 and 0.457; the full policy reaches 0.696 and 0.462. Third, the result is reproducible from one command, with seven metrics, case-cluster confidence intervals, a hash-checked vendored scorer, and a manifest that records the adapter fingerprint and the LLM mode.

Scope. The reported quantities are question-budget deltas on the public CN split under a fixed 10-turn budget. The gate itself is tested in Section 5.1; this appendix does not re-test the element-schema gate, LLM fact extraction, version-aware corpus, verifier, or memory revision. The real-agent case study in A.8 shows why the schema boundary matters.

Coverage is uneven: company, labour and tort are near the ceiling; unfair competition loses 51 facts and administrative law 14. The cause is not a missing question: of the 103 missed facts, 56 carry no topic label in the offline client's keyword taxonomy, so no question can elicit them (case_cn_0029: the platform probe is asked, the client falls back). Coverage therefore has a ceiling independent of the lawyer policy.
